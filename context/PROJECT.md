## 1. Candidate Problem  
`20 pts`

> Two sentences. One problem from your own experience: your major, a job, a campus
> process, a family business, a team. Pick something you actually care about.

* As a CS Major in my last semester job searching, I am unsure how much to invest learning on using different tools and frameworks used by a data and software engineer. Companies hiring recent graduates are expecting them to have experience with a tech stack equivalent to someone with a few years of experience , while college curricula are yet to adapt with the updated standard.

## 2. Wickedness Test  
`25 pts`

> Evaluate your problem against at least FOUR characteristics from Session 1A.
> One short paragraph or four labeled bullets. End with an honest verdict.
>
> Available characteristics: hard to formulate a problem statement; no stopping rule;
> solutions are good or bad rather than true or false; no immediate or ultimate test;
> one-shot solutions with no trial and error; no exhaustively describable solution set;
> may be a symptom of another problem; the discrepancy has multiple valid explanations;
> the problem solver is liable for consequences.
>
> "This is actually a tame problem" is a valid verdict when the reasoning is sound.

* No immediate or ultimate test - Industry tools are changing very rapidly, and different companies use different tools. For example my internship used Microsoft Azure as a cloud provider, some other companies use AWS and GCP. There is a chance some new cloud provider comes out. Cloud computing was not even a thing 20 years ago. 
* No exhaustively describable solution set - I can try mastering everything the job description wants ( for example for a data engineer role stuff like Databricks, Snowflake, LangChain, LangGraph, Docker, etc), but something new can come out soon and the employer can change the job description to meet industry standards, which means I will have to re learn
* The problem solver is liable for consequences - If i do not meet the company's job requirements, companies will not hire me, leaving me with no job after graduation, and it makes me liable since I am responsible for making sure I meet job requirements. It is not on the college .
* Solutions are good or bad rather than true or false - There are many sets of tools to master on your own, or even do an internship (which again is very hard to land in this job market and falls under the same umbrella as this problem) . What set tools to master depend on the specific job I am applying to, and can be a good choice or bad choice. Software tools used today are a good choice compared to legacy tools which will be considered a bad choice (however Informatica is still being used today in some places so there are some exceptions.) 

**Verdict: This is a wicked problem because there is no permanent solution to the skills gap. Students must continuously adapt as technologies, employer expectations, and job requirements change.**

## 3. Three Framings  
`30 pts`

> Three DISTINCT problem statements for the same situation. The test: a reader should
> not be able to swap two of them without changing what would get built.

**Framing A (the obvious one): Students are struggling to get a job in the tech industry due to a gap in skills used by employers.**

**Framing B (stakeholder shift): Employers need people who can contribute to current projects with minimal additional training.**

**Framing C (goal shift): Students need to develop the ability to continuously learn and adapt to new technologies as employer expectations change.**

---

## 4. Commit and Justify  
`included in Framings`

> Pick one framing. Defend it in a short paragraph. Then name what your choice makes
> invisible. Every framing forecloses something.

**Chosen framing: Framing A**

**Why: Universities generally focus on teaching foundational concepts that remain relevant for many years, rather than constantly changing their curricula to match every new tool used in industry. This can leave students with strong theoretical and foundational knowledge but less experience applying that knowledge to the specific technologies employers currently expect. I chose this framing because it directly focuses on the challenge I am experiencing as a graduating CS student: determining whether my current skills are enough to compete for jobs in an industry with constantly changing technical requirements.   **

**What this framing makes invisible: Students can learn new tools independently using the foundational knowledge they developed through their college courses. It makes the responsibility employers have to train and develop new graduates not visible.**
